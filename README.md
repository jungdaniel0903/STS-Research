# STS-Research
These Python notebooks were utilized to train Random Forest ML models on National Bridge Inventory data and to run every statistical test reported in the paper.

- `deterioration_model.ipynb`: deterioration model trained on 65,204 bridge-window records from Texas, Louisiana, and Puerto Rico. Compares the four deterioration targets and the rarity controls (H1), validates on unseen counties and forward in time, and injects inspector noise (H2).
- `search_corrected.ipynb`: high-risk model. Scores the 45 held-out Guam and U.S. Virgin Islands bridges (H4) and runs the 49-state leave-one-state-out analysis of label-free transfer estimates (H3).

# The Research Abstract is as follows:
Bridge deterioration models are built where inspection records are plentiful but needed where they are scarce. Using National Bridge Inventory data, this study examined what limits accuracy and how to judge whether a model can be trusted elsewhere. A Random Forest trained on 65,204 records from Texas, Louisiana, and Puerto Rico was evaluated under four definitions of deterioration with all else fixed. Predicting a one-point rating drop yielded an AUC of 0.78, whereas predicting entry into federal poor condition yielded 0.92 and held at 0.90 on unseen counties and 0.88 on future years. Adding noise matching federal findings that only 68% of inspector ratings agree within one point reduced it to 0.76, suggesting that label noise, not model design, sets the accuracy ceiling. On 45 held-out bridges from Guam and the U.S. Virgin Islands, a high-risk model ranked high-risk bridges above chance (AUC 0.81, p = 0.002), though only nine were high-risk. Across 49 held-out states, the model’s calibrated confidence predicted transfer accuracy (ρ = 0.38, p = 0.007), while distance from the training data did not. A model’s reach depends more on outcome definition and label reliability than on its sophistication.
